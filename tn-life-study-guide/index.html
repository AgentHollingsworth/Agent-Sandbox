CH 1Basic Principles of Life & Health Insurance▼
~0-1 Q · minimal

1. The Concept of Insurance

  • Insurance = the transfer of risk from one party to another through a legal contract. You pay a small premium; the insurer pools premiums from many policyholders to spread the risk.
  • Principle of indemnity = restore the insured to the same financial position they were in before the loss. You cannot profit from insurance.
  • Most accident/health/property/casualty policies = contracts of indemnity (reimburse an actual loss).
  • Life insurance = a "valued contract": it pays a predetermined amount regardless of actual loss, and creates an instant estate at death.
  • Annuities do the opposite of life insurance: they protect against outliving your income by paying a lifetime income stream.
EXAM TIP!Insurance is the transfer of risk from one party to another in exchange for the payment of premiums.

2. Types of Insurance Companies

A) Private insurers

  • Multiline insurer = sells more than one line; monoline = sells only one line.
  • Stock company — owned by stockholders/investors; organized to make a profit. Board of directors is elected by stockholders. Issues nonparticipating policies (policy owners get no dividends and no ownership/voting rights). Shareholder dividends are taxed like capital gains.
  • Mutual company — no stockholders; owned by the policy owners. Policy owners vote for the board and receive policy dividends from the divisible surplus. Mutual dividends are never guaranteed and are not taxable (return of premium). Policies are participating ("par").
  • Mutualization = stock company → mutual. Demutualization = mutual → stock (policyholders get shares).
  • Mixed plan = a company that issues both participating and nonparticipating policies.
  • Fraternal benefit society — social/charitable org that insures its members. Must be non-profit, have a lodge system with ritualistic work and elected officers, and exist for reasons other than insurance (e.g., Knights of Columbus, Independent Order of Foresters).
  • Reciprocal insurer — unincorporated; members (subscribers) insure each other (risk-sharing, not transfer). Run by an attorney-in-fact.
  • Assessment mutual — pure assessment = members billed after losses (no advance premium); advance premium assessment = premium up front, extra assessment if losses exceed it.
  • Captive insurer — owned by a parent company to insure the parent's own risks.
  • Risk Retention Group (RRG) — liability coverage for members in the same profession; created under the federal Liability Risk Retention Act (LRRA) of 1986; licensed in only one state.
  • Risk Purchasing Group (RPG) — buys insurance from an insurer for its members (doesn't act as an insurer); becomes a master policyholder.
  • Reinsurer — insures other insurers. The ceding company (primary) transfers risk to the assuming company (reinsurer). Retained portion = net retention.
  • Treaty reinsurance = automatic, broad, pre-set criteria. Facultative reinsurance = case-by-case for a specific risk.
  • Surplus lines — non-traditional market for unusual/hard-to-place risks (e.g., hole-in-one coverage). Sold via excess/surplus lines brokers; fewer consumer protections; usually must fail the standard market first.
  • Lloyd's of London — NOT an insurer; a syndicate/marketplace (like a stock exchange) where members individually underwrite risks.
  • Service providers — sell services, not insurance: HMO (prepaid, uses specific network doctors/hospitals, stresses prevention, both finances and provides care) and PPO (network discounts; can go out of network). Buyers = subscribers.
  • Industrial (home service/debit) — small face amounts (~$1,000-$2,000); agent collects premiums weekly at the home.

B) Government insurance ("social insurance")

  • Covers catastrophic/uninsurable perils or competes with private insurers. Examples: OASDI (Social Security), Medicare, Medicaid, SGLI/VGLI, NFIP (flood), FCIC (crop). (SGLI = up to $500,000 for active military in $50,000 increments.)

C) Self-insurance

  • NOT risk transfer, but NOT "going bare" either. A self-funded plan covers losses up to a threshold and buys stop-loss insurance for larger losses. Common for large companies' health/pension plans.
EXAM TIP!Participating policies let policyholders participate (elect the board + receive dividends from the divisible surplus) — issued by MUTUAL companies. Nonparticipating policies don't — issued by STOCK companies. A company issuing both = a mixed plan.
EXAM TIP!Reinsurance = "insurance for insurance companies." Treaty = broad and automatic. Facultative = tailored to specific risks, case-by-case. The ceding (original) insurer stays responsible to the client.

3. Insurer Classifications

By authorization (permission to operate in a state)

  • Authorized / Admitted = holds a certificate of authority to do business in that state.
  • Unauthorized / Nonadmitted = no certificate; generally prohibited (except limited surplus lines).

By domicile (where it's incorporated)

  • Domestic = incorporated in this state.
  • Foreign = incorporated in another U.S. state.
  • Alien = incorporated in another country.
EXAM TIP!A company from Puerto Rico is domestic in PR but a foreign insurer when authorized in Florida. A Tokyo-based company authorized in the U.S. is an alien insurer.

4. Key Departments & People

  • Producers (must act as fiduciaries): Agents (represent one or more insurers), Brokers (represent themselves/the insured), Solicitors (not licensed to sell; only find/refer prospects), Service representatives (insurance-company employees, no commissions, usually unlicensed).
  • Underwriters — identify, assess, and classify risk; approve or decline applications and set the premium.
  • Actuaries — calculate rates, reserves, and dividends (the statisticians).
  • Adjusters — investigate and settle/deny claims. A public adjuster works for the insured.

5. How Insurance Is Sold (distribution systems)

  • Career/captive agency = agents represent one company.
  • Independent agency = agents represent multiple companies.
  • Personal Producing General Agency (PPGA) = sales-focused.
  • Direct = company sells straight to consumers.

6. The Evolution of Industry Oversight (insurance is STATE-regulated)

  • Paul v. Virginia (1868) — insurance is not interstate commerce → state regulation.
  • McCarran-Ferguson Act (1945) — confirmed state regulation (limited federal role).
  • Fair Credit Reporting Act (1970) — rules for consumer/credit information.
  • Gramm-Leach-Bliley / Financial Services Modernization Act (1999) — financial privacy protections.
  • Do-Not-Call (2003) and CAN-SPAM (2003) — telemarketing/email rules.
  • Organizations: NAIC (writes model laws, no direct regulatory power), NCOIL (insurance legislators), NAIFA/NAHU (agent/producer associations), rating services like A.M. Best (grade insurer financial strength).

Chapter 1 — Quick-recall checklist

  • Insurance = transfer of risk for a premium; life insurance = valued contract (not indemnity).
  • Stock = nonparticipating (stockholder-owned). Mutual = participating (policyholder-owned, tax-free dividends). Both = mixed plan.
  • Reinsurance = insurance for insurers (treaty = automatic, facultative = case-by-case; ceding insurer stays liable).
  • Lloyd's of London is NOT an insurer — it's a marketplace.
  • Admitted/authorized = has a certificate of authority. Domestic/Foreign/Alien = this state / another state / another country.
  • Agent = represents insurer; Broker = represents buyer; Solicitor = can't sell, only refers.
  • Insurance is state-regulated (Paul v. Virginia, McCarran-Ferguson). NAIC = model laws, no direct power.
CH 2The Nature of Insurance▼
~0-1 Q · minimal
Exam weight: LOW as its own topic, but the risk/peril/hazard vocabulary here is classic multiple-choice fodder and feeds into questions across the whole test. Learn the definitions and the trap-pairs; skip the deep narrative.

1. How Insurance Works

  • Insurance = transfer of risk from one party to another for a premium.
  • Risk pooling (loss sharing) spreads the cost of losses over many similar (homogeneous) exposure units. Requirements: (1) a large number of units, (2) units face similar risks, (3) losses are accidental/unintentional, (4) units are independent.
  • Benefit: policyholders trade financial uncertainty for a known premium; insurers use statistics to predict losses.
EXAM TIP!Risk pooling transfers risk from an individual to a group; the exposure units must be homogeneous (similar); pooling + the law of large numbers = how insurers predict losses and price premiums.

2. Adverse Selection ⭐

  • Adverse selection = "selection against the insurer": higher-risk people are more likely to seek/keep coverage.
  • Controlled by: underwriting, waiting periods, pre-existing condition limitations, requiring truthful/complete health info, and group coverage.
EXAM TIP!Adverse selection increases the insurer's risk and claims cost. Concealing material info on an application is a form of adverse selection.

Warning signs of adverse selection (from the course chart)

  • Application: unusual urgency, incomplete info, coverage far above need (e.g., rushing to buy before a medical procedure) → prevented by thorough underwriting.
  • Claims: early claims or a pattern of claims (a big claim right after the policy takes effect) → prevented by waiting periods / pre-existing clauses.
  • Risk profile: concealed info, coverage well beyond financial need → prevented by risk classification, medical exams, financial verification.

3. Law of Large Numbers

As the number of similar and independent exposure units increases, actual losses get closer to expected losses. Three conditions: Independence + Similarity + Large number.

EXAM TIP!"Large numbers" alone isn't enough, units must be numerous AND independent. (A big group of homes in one flood zone isn't independent.)

4. Principle of Indemnity

  • Indemnity = restore the insured to their pre-loss financial position; no profit from a loss.
  • Health/property/casualty = indemnity contracts. Life insurance = a VALUED contract (pays a set amount) — the exception.

5. Perils, Loss, and Hazards ⭐ (trap-heavy)

  • Peril = the CAUSE of a loss (fire, accident, illness). For life insurance/annuities the peril is mortality (life = premature death; annuity = living too long).
  • Named/specified perils policy = covers ONLY the perils listed. Open/special perils policy = covers everything EXCEPT what's excluded.
  • Loss = an unintended reduction in value. Direct loss (peril causes it directly) vs Indirect/consequential loss (results from a direct loss).
  • Accident = sudden, specific time and place. Occurrence = broader (includes gradual exposure).
EXAM TIP!Every accident is an occurrence, but not every occurrence is an accident.

The three hazards (memorize the distinction) ⭐

  • Physical hazard = a tangible/observable condition (faulty wiring, icy road, poor health).
  • Moral hazard = intentional/dishonest character (disconnecting a sprinkler to file a claim, insurance fraud).
  • Morale hazard = careless/indifferent attitude because you have insurance (not replacing smoke-detector batteries).

Life & health-specific examples (from the course chart)

  • Physical: high blood pressure/obesity, a dangerous occupation (mining, construction), hazardous hobbies (skydiving, racing), poor air quality. → Can be seen/measured/documented via medical exams or records.
  • Moral: lying on the application, faking symptoms for disability benefits, submitting false claims, concealing pre-existing conditions. → Always involves dishonesty/intentional acts.
  • Morale: skipping preventive care because "insurance will pay anyway," not following the treatment plan, extending hospital stays, neglecting healthy choices. → Unintentional, from carelessness.
EXAM TIP!Physical = "can be measured/observed." Moral = "intentional and dishonest." Morale = "careless because insured."

6. Risk ⭐

  • Risk = the uncertainty of loss. Two types:
  • Pure risk = chance of loss only → INSURABLE (death, illness, injury).
  • Speculative risk = chance of loss OR gain → NOT insurable (investing, gambling).
EXAM TIP!Ask "Can this result in a gain?" If yes → speculative (not insurable). If no → pure (insurable). Insurers only insure pure risk.

Elements of an insurable risk

Loss must be: due to chance (accidental), definite and measurable, predictable, NOT catastrophic (from the insurer's view), part of a large number of similar exposure units, and the premium must be economically feasible.

Risk classifications

Preferred (better than average, lower premium), Standard (average), Substandard/rated (higher risk, higher premium or declined).

7. Handling Risk — "STARR" (+ Prevention) ⭐

  • Sharing (e.g., coinsurance 80/20; reciprocal exchanges) — best when multiple parties can share the risk.
  • Transfer (insurance/reinsurance — the main one) — best when loss potential is large but cost to transfer is reasonable.
  • Avoidance (eliminate the activity) — the most complete form; best when the risk is too high/severe.
  • Reduction (lower likelihood/severity — smoke alarms, sprinklers) — best when a risk can't be avoided.
  • Retention (deductibles, self-insurance) — best when losses are small, frequent, and predictable.
  • plus Prevention (actions to eliminate loss — masonry vs wood, de-icing a plane).
  • Self-insurance is NOT "no insurance" — it's a planned strategy of holding reserves.
EXAM TIP!Remember the risk-handling methods with STARR (Sharing, Transfer, Avoidance, Reduction, Retention). Avoidance = the most complete form of risk management. Reinsurance = a risk-transfer method insurers use to avoid catastrophic loss.

In this chapter, we explored the fundamental concepts that make insurance work. We learned that insurance is essentially a method of transferring risk from individuals to a larger group through risk pooling, which makes uncertain losses more predictable and manageable.

We discovered that only pure risks (those with potential for loss only) are insurable, while speculative risks (those with potential for both loss and gain) are not. We examined how different types of hazards — physical, moral, and morale — can increase the likelihood of loss, and how insurance companies work to manage these risks.

The law of large numbers emerged as a crucial principle, showing how insurance companies can predict losses more accurately when they insure many similar risks. We also learned about adverse selection and the various methods insurance companies use to combat it, including underwriting and waiting periods.

The chapter covered the six primary methods of handling risk: sharing, transfer, avoidance, reduction, retention, and prevention. Each method serves specific purposes in risk management, with insurance being a prime example of risk transfer.

Finally, we explored the principle of indemnity, which ensures that insurance serves its intended purpose of restoring insureds to their pre-loss financial position without allowing for profit from losses.

Chapter 2 — Quick-recall checklist

  • Insurance = transfer of risk; risk pooling needs homogeneous, independent, numerous, accidental units.
  • Adverse selection = selection against the insurer (concealment counts); fought with underwriting.
  • Law of large numbers = number AND independence.
  • Peril = cause of loss (life/annuity peril = mortality). Named = only listed; Open = all but excluded.

CH 3Legal Concepts of Insurance▼
part of 12 Q · medium
Exam weight: MEDIUM-HIGH. Contract law is explicitly named in the TN outline (III.D: elements of a contract + the unique aspects conditional/unilateral/adhesion/aleatory), and insurable interest is named under III.B Underwriting. These are guaranteed points. Learn this section properly.

1. The Four Elements of a Valid Contract — "CLOC" ⭐

  • Competent parties — both sides must have legal capacity. NOT competent: minors (except for necessities), the mentally incompetent, people under the influence of drugs/alcohol at application, those coerced, enemy aliens, and (per state law) convicts. The insurer is competent if licensed/authorized in the state.
  • Legal purpose — must be lawful and not against public policy. Insurance contracts are always deemed to have legal purpose.
  • Offer and acceptance — the applicant makes the offer by submitting a completed application + the initial premium. An application without premium is only an invitation, not an offer. Acceptance = the insurer issuing the policy (or the producer delivering it). A counteroffer by the insurer voids the original offer.
  • Consideration — applicant gives premium + truthful statements; insurer gives its promise to pay. Consideration is the "binding force" and must be perpetual (ongoing premiums).
EXAM TIP!The two parties to an insurance contract are the policy owner (applicant) and the insurer. The beneficiary and the insured are NOT parties to the contract. If a question asks who contracts with the insurer, answer "the policy owner" even if "the insured" is offered.
EXAM TIP!BOTH parties must be competent. If one party is a minor or mentally incompetent, the contract is VOID from the beginning, not voidable.

2. Unique Characteristics of Insurance Contracts ⭐

  • Aleatory — unequal exchange; benefits depend on chance. You might pay $2,400 in premiums and collect $500,000.
  • Adhesion — written entirely by the insurer; "take it or leave it." Therefore ambiguities are interpreted in favor of the insured. Doctrine of reasonable expectations: the insured gets the coverage a sensible, prudent person would expect the policy to provide.
  • Unilateral — only ONE party (the insurer) makes a legally enforceable promise. The applicant never promises to pay premiums; the insurer's only remedy is to cancel.
  • Conditional — benefits are owed only if conditions are met (pay premiums, give notice, provide proof of loss).
  • Personal — a contract between the insurer and a specific person, insuring the owner, not the property. Generally cannot be transferred.
⚠️ EXCEPTIONLife insurance is NOT a personal contract — it CAN be assigned to a new owner. (That's why we say "policy owner," not "policyholder.")

3. Valued vs. Indemnity Contracts ⭐

  • Valued contract — pays a stated/predetermined sum regardless of actual loss. Life insurance and AD&D are valued contracts.
  • Indemnity contract — pays an amount equal to the actual loss, restoring you to your pre-loss position (most health/property/casualty; also fixed-per-day disability benefits).
EXAM TIP!Life insurance is ALWAYS a valued contract — you can't calculate the "actual loss" of a life.

4. Insurable Interest ⭐ (also tested under Underwriting)

  • Rule of thumb: "You can only insure what you could lose money on."
  • Automatically exists in: yourself (unlimited), your spouse, your children/parents, a business & its key employee, and a creditor-debtor relationship. Also a niece/nephew living in your household or under your guardianship.
  • Cannot insure: a neighbor's house, your mail carrier's life, a stranger, a car you don't own.
  • TIMING (critical): Life/health insurance → insurable interest needed ONLY at the time of application. It does NOT need to exist at claim time. (Divorced spouses may keep existing policies, but couldn't buy new ones on each other.) Property/casualty → needed at application AND at the time of loss.
  • STOLI (stranger-originated life insurance) has no insurable interest, lacks legal purpose, and is unenforceable/illegal.

5. Utmost Good Faith: Warranty vs. Representation vs. Concealment ⭐

  • Utmost good faith — both parties must deal honestly and disclose all material facts.
  • Warranty = a statement guaranteed to be true; becomes part of the contract; presumed material; must be exactly true. (Mostly property/casualty.)
  • Representation = a statement believed to be true to the best of the applicant's knowledge; NOT part of the contract; must only be substantially true. Statements on life/health applications are representations, not warranties. A false one that would have influenced the insurer = material misrepresentation.
  • Concealment = failure to disclose a known, material fact. The insurer must prove concealment AND materiality. Grounds for rescission (voiding the contract) whether intentional or not.
EXAM TIP!Warranty = must be exactly true throughout → breach can void the policy. Representation = must be substantially true when made → only a material misrepresentation makes it voidable.

6. Void vs. Voidable, Cancellation & Fraud ⭐

  • Void = never a contract at all (missing a CLOC element). Unenforceable by either party. Example: a 16-year-old buys a policy — void from the start.
  • Voidable = a valid contract that one party may set aside. Example: policy owner stops paying premiums; the insurer may terminate.
  • Cancellation = voluntary termination; the policy owner may cancel anytime. A policy lapses if premiums aren't paid by the end of the grace period.
  • Fraud = intentional deceit for gain. Note: after the 2-year contestable period, life insurers generally cannot void the policy for misrepresentation/concealment.

7. Waiver, Parole Evidence Rule, and Estoppel ⭐

  • Waiver = the voluntary surrender of a known right by the insurer. If the insurer doesn't enforce a provision, it can't later deny a claim on it. (Includes "waiver by silence" — failing to act within a reasonable time.)
  • Parole evidence rule = only the written policy is binding. Verbal promises made BEFORE the policy was issued cannot override the written terms. Changes must be made in writing via endorsement.
  • Estoppel = the insurer is prevented from denying a claim when the insured relied on an agent's representation. All FOUR conditions must be met: (1) the agent makes a representation (within their authority), (2) the client believes it, (3) the client acts/relies on it, (4) the client suffers financial harm.
EXAM TIP!Memory aid: Parole evidence = PAST (statements before the policy; the written policy wins). Estoppel = EXISTING policy (statements after it's in force; the insurer must honor the agent's word).

8. The Law of Agency & Agent Authority ⭐

The insurer is the principal; the agent acts on its behalf. Acts of the agent (within authority) are acts of the insurer, payments to the agent are payments to the insurer, and the agent's knowledge is presumed known by the insurer.

  • Express = specifically written in the agent's contract.
  • Implied = not written but necessary to do the job (e.g., printing business cards).
  • Apparent = created by the principal's own actions (company email, business cards, portal access) that lead the public to reasonably believe authority exists.
EXAM TIP!Express = written in the contract. Implied = necessary to do the job. Apparent = what customers would reasonably assume. Most agent mistakes that create company liability fall under apparent authority.

9. Producers: Agents, Brokers, Solicitors, Fiduciaries

  • Agent represents the insurer and CAN bind coverage.
  • Broker represents the buyer/applicant and CANNOT bind coverage (must place business through an agent/insurer).
  • Solicitor may only seek out applicants; cannot bind.
  • Fiduciary = holds a position of financial trust. Producers act as fiduciaries when handling premiums or advising on financial security. (Never commingle client funds with personal funds.)

10. Other Legal Concepts

  • Subrogation = after paying a claim, the insurer "steps into the insured's shoes" to recover from the at-fault party. (Property/health, not life.)
  • Tort law = private/civil wrongs, heard in civil court (vs. criminal law = wrongs against society).
  • Simple negligence = failing to act reasonably (agent forgets to submit an application).
  • Gross negligence = reckless disregard (agent never maintains records).
  • Willful and wanton = knows harm will likely occur → NOT covered by insurance.
  • E&O (Errors & Omissions) = professional liability insurance for producers; pays defense costs + damages for negligent professional service. Covered: failing to place requested coverage, administrative/premium-calculation errors, misstating coverage, failing to make a requested policy change, not explaining provisions, failing to recommend coverage. Excluded: criminal, illegal, dishonest, malicious, or intentional acts, plus libel/slander.

Finally, we covered tort law and the importance of E&O insurance, understanding how these concepts protect both insurance professionals and their clients.

HEAVIEST CHAPTER. Exam weight: HIGHEST. This is Section I of the TN outline — 15 scored questions (whole life, interest/market-sensitive, term, annuities, combination plans). Together with Ch 5 this is ~44% of your exam. Learn every product's death benefit / premium / cash value behavior cold.

1. General Concepts

  • Life insurance = transfer of the risk of premature death; it creates an immediate estate at death.
  • There are no "standard" life insurance policies (unlike property & casualty).
  • Categories: permanent vs. temporary, group vs. individual, fixed vs. variable.
  • Term = protection IF you die too soon. Whole life = protection WHEN you die.

2. TERM (Temporary) Life Insurance ⭐

Core traits: pure/temporary protection · most coverage for the lowest initial premium · NO cash value · expires with nothing paid if you outlive it · premiums are level during the term.

Types by death-benefit behavior

  • Level term — death benefit stays constant. (Most common.)
  • Decreasing term — death benefit declines to $0; premium stays level. Used for debt.
    • Mortgage redemption insurance = decreasing term matched to the mortgage balance.
    • Credit life = decreasing term covering a debtor; beneficiary is the lender; max benefit = the loan amount; usually ≤10 years; borrower pays. A lender cannot force you to buy it from a specific company (that's illegal coercion).
  • Increasing term — death benefit rises over the term (often tied to CPI/cost-of-living); premium level. Often sold as a rider.

Options (must be built in at purchase; cannot be added later)

  • Renewable — renew without evidence of insurability. Renewal is at attained age, so the premium always increases ("step-up premium").
  • ART / YRT (annually/yearly renewable term) = 1-year coverage, renew each year, no proof of insurability. The most basic form of life insurance.
  • Re-entry term — renew at standard rates, OR take a medical exam to qualify for lower rates.
  • Convertible — convert to whole life/permanent without evidence of insurability.
    • Attained age = premium based on age at conversion (the default/usual method, higher premium).
    • Original age = premium based on the original issue age (lower premium, but you must pay the difference in cost up front). Attractive only in the early years.
  • Interim term = convertible term for someone who needs coverage now but can't afford permanent yet; temporary premium is at original age, permanent premium at attained age.
EXAM TIP!If a question just says "a term policy" with no type specified, assume level term.
EXAM TIP!If a question involves renewing, the new premium is ALWAYS higher. It never stays the same or decreases. Only temporary coverage is renewed — permanent coverage never needs renewal.
EXAM TIP!If a conversion question doesn't specify the method, assume attained age.

Advantages / Disadvantages

  • Pros: cheapest for a given period; protects insurability if renewable/convertible; good for debts/mortgages; most protection per dollar.
  • Cons: protection ends with the term; premiums rise on renewal; few death claims are actually paid; no cash value, so it never matures.

3. PERMANENT (Whole Life) Insurance ⭐

Core traits: level/fixed death benefit · level/fixed premium · guaranteed tax-deferred cash value · lifetime protection · never needs renewing or converting.

Cash value mechanics

  • Cash value typically starts building 2-3 years after issue, grows at a guaranteed fixed rate.
  • It's a living benefit = the cash surrender value / nonforfeiture value. Owner can surrender for it, or borrow against it (loans accrue interest).
  • As cash value grows, the insurer's net amount at risk shrinks.
  • Traditionally the cash value equals the face amount at age 100 = the policy matures/endows (many newer policies use 105/115/120).
  • Cash value is not normally paid in addition to the death benefit (unless a "face amount plus cash value" rider exists).
  • Bundled premium — the insurer needn't break down where your premium goes. Priced per $1,000 of coverage.
  • The shorter the premium-paying period, the HIGHER the premium.

Nontraditional whole life variants

  • Modified whole life — lower premium for an intro period (3-10 yrs), then ONE increase to a level higher than straight life, then level for life. (Two fixed premiums.)
  • Graded premium whole life — starts lower, then premiums increase annually during the initial period, then level for life.
  • Enhanced (economatic / extraordinary) life — low-premium participating permanent; dividends buy paid-up additions or one-year term to offset a declining face amount.
  • Indeterminate premium — low initial premium; insurer may later raise or lower it based on investment/mortality/expense results, but never above the guaranteed maximum.
  • Current Assumption Whole Life (CAWL) / interest-sensitive — premiums and cash values shift with the insurer's death/investment/expense assumptions; uses an accumulation account, guaranteed minimum cash value, maximum premium, and surrender charges. Comes in low-premium (redetermination provision) and high-premium (optional pay-up provision) types.
  • Equity-indexed whole life — interest tied to an index (S&P 500) with a guaranteed minimum; NOT a security.

4. Alternative / Flexible Products ⭐

Adjustable Life

  • Combines whole life with an adjustment provision: the owner may change the face amount/coverage.
  • Adjustments are prospective (future) only. Premium is fixed for each policy year.
  • Increasing coverage requires proof of insurability. Premiums cannot be waived.

Universal Life (UL) — "flexible premium adjustable life"

  • Most flexible. Essentially annually renewable term + an accumulation account.
  • Unbundled premium — the owner gets a full breakdown (mortality charges, expenses, interest, cash value) and an annual statement.
  • Flexible premiums: only the first year's premium is required. After that the owner may pay any amount, or nothing, as long as the cash value covers the cost of insurance. If the cash value runs out, the policy lapses.
  • Target premium = the suggested premium to keep it funded.
  • Cash value earns a guaranteed minimum rate (approx. 2-4%) or a higher current rate; may be adjusted quarterly.
  • Partial withdrawals are allowed (unlike whole life, where you must borrow). Surrender charges apply roughly the first 10-15 years.
TWO DEATH BENEFIT OPTIONS — KNOW THESE COLDOption A (Option 1) = LEVEL death benefit. Decreasing term + increasing cash value. (Net amount at risk shrinks.)

Option B (Option 2) = INCREASING death benefit = face amount + cash value. Level term + increasing cash value.
  • Corridor = required "space" between death benefit and cash value; if it disappears the policy becomes a MEC and loses favorable tax treatment.
  • Riders: waiver of monthly deduction (waives only the cost of insurance, not the full premium) and no-lapse guarantee.
  • Indexed UL — return linked to an index, capped on the upside but cannot lose cash value in a downturn (just earns nothing).
  • Guaranteed / no-lapse UL — guarantees the death benefit as long as minimum premiums are paid, even if cash value hits zero.
  • Survivorship UL = second-to-die.

5. SEC-Regulated (Variable) Products ⭐

Both require a life license AND a FINRA securities license. Regulated by the SEC; FINRA oversees the firms.

  • Variable Life (VL) — FIXED premium, guaranteed minimum death benefit, cash value in a separate account invested in stocks/bonds/money market. The OWNER bears all investment risk. Cash value can go to zero; the death benefit floor is guaranteed.
  • Variable Universal Life (VUL) — hybrid: flexible premiums (like UL) + investment control (like VL). NO guaranteed minimum death benefit and no guaranteed cash value.
MEMORY HOOKVL = fixed premium + guaranteed floor. VUL = flexible premium + no guarantees. Variable = separate account = owner takes the risk.

6. Special Use Products ⭐ (combination plans are outline-named)

  • Family plan / family protection = whole life on the breadwinner + LEVEL TERM riders on spouse and children. Children's coverage is convertible without evidence of insurability, and new children are automatically added free. It does NOT terminate when the primary insured dies.
  • Family income policy = whole life + DECREASING term. Income to the beneficiary for a period measured from the policy's issue date (not from death).
  • Family maintenance policy = whole life + LEVEL term. Income for a stated period measured from the date of death, then the face amount is paid.
  • Joint life (first-to-die) — covers 2+ lives, pays on the FIRST death, then terminates. Uses averaged ages; cheaper than separate policies.
  • Survivorship / last-survivor (second-to-die) — pays on the LAST death. Used in estate planning to pay estate taxes.
  • Juvenile insurance — insures a minor; uses third-party ownership (adult owns/pays); no minor consent needed. Juvenile is generally under 15 (some states 16).
  • Jumping juvenile (junior estate builder) — face amount jumps ~5x at the age of majority (e.g., 21) with no evidence of insurability and no premium increase.
  • Industrial (home service / debit) life — small face amounts (~$1,000), premiums collected weekly/monthly at the home; often burial insurance. Monthly debit ordinary = industrial + ordinary hybrid.

7. Endowments & MECs ⭐

  • Endowment — pays the face amount at death OR at maturity, and matures before age 100 (e.g., 20-year endowment, endowment at 65). Fastest cash-value growth, highest premiums.
  • Types: standard (full at death or maturity), semi-endowment (100% at death, 50% if survive), pure endowment (pays only if you survive), juvenile endowment.
  • Tax Reform Act of 1984: policies endowing before age 95 no longer qualify as life insurance for tax purposes.
MODIFIED ENDOWMENT CONTRACT (MEC)Created by TAMRA (1988) to stop life insurance being used as a tax shelter.

· A policy is a MEC if premiums in the first 7 years exceed what would have paid the policy up in 7 years = failed the SEVEN-PAY TEST.
· ALL single-premium whole life policies are MECs.
· Once a MEC, always a MEC (can never revert).
· Tax treatment: distributions/loans/withdrawals are taxed LIFO (earnings first), plus a 10% penalty before age 59½. Death benefit stays income-tax-free.

8. Other Products & Concepts

  • Face amount plus cash value — pays face plus cash value at death; costs much more; non-standard.
  • AD&D — benefits for accidental death, dismemberment, blindness, paralysis.
  • Nonmedical life — no medical exam, more expensive (risk is averaged); medical history still asked.
  • Participating (mutual insurers) = receives dividends. Nonparticipating (stock insurers) = no dividends.
  • STOLI / IOLI — policies originated for strangers/investors with no insurable interest. Both are fraudulent and prohibited. IOLI is always investor-initiated. Legitimate life settlements are NOT STOLI.

Chapter 4 — Quick-recall checklist

  • Term = temporary, no cash value, most protection per dollar. Level / decreasing (mortgage, credit life) / increasing (CPI).
  • Renewable = no proof of insurability, premium always goes up (attained age, step-up). Convertible = to permanent, no proof; default = attained age.
  • Credit life: beneficiary = lender, max benefit = loan balance.
  • Whole life = level DB + level premium + guaranteed cash value, matures at 100. Shorter pay period = higher premium.
  • Single premium WL = most expensive up front, cheapest overall, always a MEC.
  • Modified = one premium jump. Graded = annual increases early on.
  • UL Option A = LEVEL DB; Option B = face + cash value (INCREASING). Only yr-1 premium required; partial withdrawals allowed; can lapse.
  • VL = fixed premium + guaranteed min DB. VUL = flexible premium + NO guarantees. Both need a securities license.
  • Joint life = first-to-die. Survivorship = second-to-die (estate planning).
  • Family income = decreasing term (from issue date); family maintenance = level term (from date of death).
  • MEC = failed the 7-pay test; taxed LIFO + 10% penalty before 59½; single-premium WL always qualifies.
  • STOLI/IOLI = no insurable interest = illegal.

1. Standard Policy Provisions ⭐

Most states require the same set of provisions in every life policy — wording is nearly identical company to company. In general, provisions protect the policy owner.

  • Entire contract provision — found at the start of the policy; the entire contract = the policy + attached copy of the application + any riders/endorsements. Nothing may be incorporated by reference (no outside documents). Prevents the insurer from later claiming it didn't give the owner the full contract, and blocks the insurer/agent from unilaterally changing the policy after issue; only authorized company officers may make changes, endorsements, riders, or amendments, and the owner must agree.
  • Execution clause — the contract is "executed" once both parties (insurer + owner) have satisfied their conditions.
  • Changes provision — any change must be in writing, endorsed/attached to the policy, and only an executive officer or authorized home-office personnel can make or waive a provision. A producer/agent does NOT need to countersign.
  • Privilege of change clause (policy change / conversion provision) — governs changing coverage. Premium increasing, face amount same → no proof of insurability needed. Premium decreasing OR face amount increasing → proof of insurability required (prevents adverse selection).
  • Insuring clause/agreement — the company's fundamental promise to pay; appears on the first page (the "policy face"), signed by the president & secretary.
  • Consideration clause — the owner's consideration = completing the application + paying the initial premium (+ truthful representations). If consideration fails (bounced check, material misrepresentation discovered), the contract is void — never valid; premiums returned.
EXAM TIP!"Entire contract" = what's IN the contract (no outside references). "Consideration" = what the owner GIVES to make it binding (app + truth).

2. Incontestability and Misstatement of Age/Sex ⭐⭐ (guaranteed points)

  • Incontestable clause — after a set period (usually 2 years from issue), the insurer can no longer contest the policy's validity (fraud, material misstatement, concealment) as long as it's in force. Applies to the face amount + any death riders; generally does NOT apply to accidental death or disability provisions (insurers reserve the right to investigate further).
THREE EXCEPTIONS — insurer CAN still void at any time, even past the contestable period· Impersonation/identity fraud — someone else signed the app or took the medical exam.
· Lack of insurable interest at time of application.
· Intent to murder the insured for proceeds.

The insurer can always void/cancel for non-payment of premiums, contestable period or not.
  • Misstatement of age or sex provision — the policy is NEVER voided for a misstated age/sex. Instead, the death benefit is adjusted to what the premium paid would have bought at the correct age/sex.
  • Worked example (memorize the method): Dave, actual age 35, is issued a $10,000 policy for $100/yr. At his death it's discovered his true age at application was 40 (premium should've been $125). Adjustment = 100/125 = 4/5 → insurer pays $8,000 (4/5 of $10,000).
EXAM TIP!Incontestable = protects against the insurer voiding for fraud/misrepresentation after 2 years. Misstatement of age or sex — the policy is NEVER VOIDED for a misstated age/sex. Instead, the death benefit is adjusted, never a void, regardless of when it's discovered (intentional or not).

3. Rights of Policy Ownership & Assignment ⭐

  • Owner's provision — the policy owner holds ALL rights: name/change beneficiary, borrow against cash value, select premium mode, transfer rights (assignment), and (if participating) receive dividends & vote for the board.
  • Applicant control clause — if the insured is a minor, a parent/guardian can be given full control until the minor reaches a stated age.
  • Primary rights of a policy owner (full list): assign/change beneficiaries; choose settlement options; terminate the policy and pick a non-forfeiture option; change the premium schedule; assign ownership of the policy to someone else; decide what happens with dividends; convert/renew a term policy if applicable; exercise any other policy options.
  • Assignment provision — lets the owner transfer their rights to someone else (assignment); the prior owner = assignor, new owner = assignee. The insurer's permission is not required, but the owner must give written notice. Insurable interest is NOT required between insured and assignee.
  • Absolute (complete/voluntary) assignment — ALL ownership rights transfer; generally cannot be recovered by the assignor.
  • Collateral (conditional) assignment — only some rights transfer, and only temporarily, usually to secure a loan (e.g., cash value pledged to a bank). The assignee/bank is a beneficiary only up to the loan balance; anything above that still goes to the named beneficiary.

Beneficiary provisions

  • Revocable beneficiary — owner can change anytime, one's own permission needed.
  • Irrevocable beneficiary — cannot be changed without the beneficiary's consent; an irrevocable beneficiary must also agree to any assignment. The owner generally cannot change an irrevocable beneficiary (but CAN change a revocable one).
EXAM TIP!Absolute = ALL rights, permanent. Collateral/conditional = SOME rights, temporary, tied to a debt amount.

4. Free-Look, Premium Mode, Grace Period & Reinstatement ⭐

  • Free-look ("right to examine") provision — owner can review a new policy after delivery and return it for a full premium refund, no reason needed. Required on all life policies except aviation/flight insurance. Typically 10 days from delivery (varies by state; some states extend it for seniors starting the day after delivery).
  • Mode of premium provision — owner picks payment frequency. Annual = cheapest (lowest admin cost, savings passed to owner). Monthly = most expensive (highest admin cost).
  • Grace period — time after the due date the policy stays in force if unpaid. Life insurance is usually 30 days/1 month. If insured dies during the grace period before paying, beneficiaries get the face amount minus the owed premium. Purpose (along with reinstatement/APL): keep the policy in force without re-proving insurability or paying an older-age rate.
  • Reinstatement — if a policy lapses, it can generally be reinstated within 3 years. Requires: proof of insurability/good health + payment of all back premiums + interest. A new two-year contestable period starts for reinstatement-application statements, but there is NO new suicide exclusion period. A surrendered policy can never be reinstated.
EXAM TIP!Free-look = brand-new policy, full refund, no reason needed. Reinstatement = lapsed policy, needs proof of insurability + back premiums, new contestable period but NOT a new suicide period.

5. Cash Value Provisions & Options ⭐⭐ (heavily tested)

  • Excess interest provision — cash value grows faster than the guaranteed rate if the insurer earns more. Credited via the index-linked method (tied to an economic indicator like CPI) or the portfolio method (tied to the insurer's own investment earnings).

Policy loans

  • Policy loan provision — required in all whole life policies; owner can borrow cash value at any time; owner can repay anytime; no credit check needed — the cash value itself is the collateral (technically a collateral assignment of the cash value).
  • Max loan: cash value minus projected interest. Unpaid loan + interest reduces the death benefit at claim, and reduces cash value available at surrender.
  • Interest rates: most states cap fixed rates at 8-10%; variable rates may be tied to an index (e.g., Moody's Corporate Bond Index) and reset yearly. Unpaid interest is added to the loan balance; if the loan + interest ever exceeds the cash value, the policy lapses.
  • Loans are generally not taxable while the policy stays in force. If the policy lapses/surrenders with an outstanding loan greater than premiums paid, the excess (gain) is taxed as ordinary income. Loan interest paid is NOT tax-deductible.
  • Insurers may defer a loan/cash-value payout for up to 6 months in most states — this deferral does NOT apply to death claims or automatic premium loan payments.
  • Automatic Premium Loan (APL) — optional provision; if a premium is missed and the grace period expires, the insurer automatically loans against cash value to cover it. Requires sufficient cash value; can eventually deplete the policy and cause a lapse anyway.

Non-forfeiture options (the "3 ways to surrender") ⭐⭐

Standard non-forfeiture laws guarantee a policy owner won't lose 100% of built-up equity just because they stop paying. Values available after 3 years. Once surrendered, a policy can never be reinstated.

6. Provisions & Options Related to Policy Proceeds

  • Settlement options provision — defines how death proceeds can be paid; lump sum = the principal/default method, paid income-tax-free. Any other option keeps money with the insurer, who must pay interest — that interest is taxable as ordinary income.
  • Beneficiary designation — owner's right, part of the entire contract; beneficiaries don't sign the app or need to be notified. Owner can change anytime unless the beneficiary is irrevocable.
  • Spendthrift clause — protects proceeds from a beneficiary's creditors and their own poor decisions; only applies to non-lump-sum settlement options; allow full or limited annual withdrawal.
  • Withdrawal provision — governs pulling out funds left on deposit with the insurer under an installment settlement; may allow full or limited annual withdrawal.
  • Accelerated (living) benefits provision/rider — lets a terminally ill person (physician-certified, generally 12-24 months or less to live) receive part of the death benefit while alive, income-tax-free. Amount paid reduces the death benefit dollar-for-dollar. Insurer must disclose effects on cash value, premiums, Medicaid/government benefits, and taxes.
  • Catastrophic/chronic/critical illness (dread disease) rider — similar, but the covered disease must be named in the policy (cancer, heart disease, renal failure, stroke, etc.), or benefits may trigger from inability to perform 2+ Activities of Daily Living (ADLs).
  • Long-term care (LTC) rider — pays if the insured is confined and needs long-term care; face amount/cash value NOT reduced.
    • Generalized/independent design — LTC benefit is separate; face amount/cash value NOT reduced.
    • Integrated design — LTC payouts DO reduce the eventual death benefit dollar-for-dollar. (The one exception that does reduce it.)

8. Life Insurance Policy Riders ⭐⭐ (long list — learn by function)

Disability-related riders

  • Waiver of premium rider — if insured becomes totally disabled (usually after 6 consecutive months), insurer waives all future premiums. Normally expires around age 60-65. Excludes self-inflicted injury, criminal activity.
  • Waiver of cost-of-insurance / waiver of monthly deductions — the UL version; waives only the mortality/cost-of-insurance charge, NOT the full flexible premium.
  • Payor benefit rider — added only to juvenile policies; waives premiums (until the child reaches age 18/21/25) if the payor (parent/guardian) dies or becomes totally disabled. Coverage, cash value, and other riders continue building normally — the child's benefits are never reduced.
  • Disability income benefit rider — pays a monthly income (commonly 1% of face amount, capped around $1,000/mo) if totally disabled. Example: $20,000 policy → $200/mo if totally disabled. Often bundled with waiver of premium.
EXAM TIP!Waiver of premium = pays the premium FOR you. Disability income rider = pays YOU an income. Payor benefit = covers premiums on a child's policy when the adult payor dies/disables.

Death-benefit-multiplier riders

  • Accidental Death Benefit (ADB) rider / "double indemnity" ⚑ garbled in the source PDF — pays a multiple of the face amount if death if from illness/complications, self-inflicted injury, riot, and private aviation. Insured must typically die within 90 days of the accident.
  • Accidental Death & Dismemberment (AD&D) rider — adds a dismemberment/severance benefit. The ADB payout = principal sum; the dismemberment payout = capital sum, usually 1/2 of the principal sum. Covers loss of limb, sight/hearing (presumptive disability).

Future-insurability riders

  • Guaranteed Insurability Option (GIO) (a.k.a. Guaranteed Purchase Option/GPO, Future Increase Option/FIO) — lets the owner buy additional coverage at future dates, WITH NO evidence of insurability. "Use it or lose it" — options can often be swapped for life events (marriage, birth/adoption of a child = "stork provision," home purchase). Milestone dates often start every 3 years, no insurability needed.
  • Cost-of-Living (COLA) rider — automatically increases the face amount based on the CPI, no evidence of insurability needed. Owner can decline an increase and keep current premium/benefit. Usually structured as an increasing term rider on whole/term policies; not typically added to UL (already flexible).

Term riders attached to a permanent policy

(A term rider can ONLY attach to a permanent policy. Coverage period can NOT outlast the base policy's premium-paying period.)

  • Level term rider — adds a fixed extra death benefit for a set period at a set cost.
  • Decreasing term rider — extra benefit declines over the period; cheaper since risk shrinks. Often tied to a mortgage/debt/decade.
  • Increasing term rider — extra benefit rises, tied to premiums paid or cash value (a type of increasing term). Full ROP term policy 100% of premiums, tax-free, if the insured survives the full term.
  • Return of Premium (ROP) rider — at death, pays face amount + all premiums ever paid.
  • Return of cash value rider — at death, pays face amount + the current cash value.

"Other insured" / family riders

(Any rider covering someone OTHER than the primary insured is always TERM insurance.)

  • Spousal (term) rider — covers the spouse.
  • Children's (term) rider — covers all children as a class, set amount per child.
  • Family (term) rider — covers the rest of the family, but not the primary insured.
EXAM TIP!A classic trap: "client wants a rider to cover their spouse in case of accidental death" → the answer is a spousal/dependent rider, NOT the accidental death rider (ADB only covers the primary insured).

Business-use rider

  • Exchange privilege (substitute/change of insured) rider — lets a business policy (e.g., covering a key employee) swap in a new insured (same amount) if the original leaves the company; premium recalculated for the new insured's age/sex.

9. Life Insurance Policy Exclusions ⭐

⚑ garbled in the source PDF Exclusions let for specific causes of death. Modern policies increasingly replace exclusions with an extra premium ("rate-up" instead of the policy) — except suicide; if it's excluded, it's excluded for the life of the policy (except suicide); if it's excluded later, it's covered forever.

  • War/military service exclusion — two versions:
    • Status clause (restrictive) — no coverage at all while in the military, even if killed on furlough.
    • Results clause — excludes only death from actual military activities/maneuvers; death on furlough (not engaged in military activity) IS covered. (Assume "results clause" unless the question says "status clause.")
  • Aviation exclusion — modern policies generally do cover commercial airline passengers/crew; exclusions target high-risk aviation only (student/stunt/test pilots, flight instructors, crop-dusting).
  • Commission of a felony / illegal occupation — excludes death of the person committing the crime (innocent victims/bystanders are always covered).
  • Intoxicants/narcotics exclusion — insurer may deny if insured was intoxicated/under the influence at time of loss.
  • Hazardous hobbies/occupations/avocations exclusion — scuba diving, high-risk jobs — increasingly replaced by a rate-up instead of an exclusion. If excluded, it becomes part of the entire contract, so it can't be added later post-claim underwriting once the contestable period ends.
  • Suicide exclusion — the only exclusion that "falls off" after a set period, generally the first 2 years. Death by suicide within the window → claim denied, premiums refunded to the estate/policy owner. After 2 years → fully covered, full face amount paid. Reinstatement starts a new contestable period but does NOT restart the suicide clock — it's still measured from the original issue date.

Chapter 5 — Quick-recall checklist

  • Entire contract = policy + application copy + riders, nothing incorporated by reference. Consideration = app + initial premium (+ truthful reps).
  • Incontestable clause = 2 years, then insurer can't contest, EXCEPT: impersonation, no insurable interest, intent to murder. Always voidable for non-payment.
  • Misstatement of age/sex — the policy is NEVER VOIDED — always just adjusts the death benefit (ratio of premium paid ÷ premium that should've been paid).
  • Owner's rights: change beneficiary, borrow, choose premium mode, assign, get dividends, vote (if participating).
  • Absolute assignment = ALL rights, permanent. Collateral/conditional assignment = SOME rights, temporary (usually loan collateral).
  • Free-look = new policy, full refund, ~10 days, no reason needed. Reinstatement = within 3 years, needs proof of insurability + back premiums + interest; new 2-yr contestable period, but NO new suicide clock.
  • Grace period = ~30 days, policy stays in force. 3 non-forfeiture options: Cash surrender (lump sum now), Reduced paid-up (smaller PERMANENT policy, no more premiums), Extended Term — this is the default.
  • Policy loans: insurer can't call, no credit check, unpaid loan + interest reduces the death benefit; loan interest not tax-deductible; insurer can defer payout up to 6 months for cash (not for death claims or APL).
  • Dividends = CRAPPO: Cash, Reduction of premium, Accumulate at interest (interest taxable), Paid-up additions (increases DB), Paid-up policy, One-year term.
  • Dividends are tax-free only up to total premiums paid — cumulative dividends exceeding cumulative premiums = excess taxable as ordinary income. Don't confuse "dividends aren't taxable" with the accumulate-at-interest exception (interest earned IS always taxable).
  • Riders: Waiver of premium (waives premium payments); Payor benefit (covers premiums on a child's policy while the payor is disabled); ADB/double indemnity (multiplies the face amount if death is accidental); GIO (buy more later, no evidence of insurability needed); COLA (tracks CPI, only increases); term riders only attach to PERMANENT policies; riders covering anyone besides the primary insured are always TERM.
  • Exclusions last for the life of the policy EXCEPT suicide, which falls off after 2 years (measured from original issue, not reinstatement).
CH 6Premiums, Proceeds & Beneficiaries ⭐▼
spans 15 + 8 Q · high value
Exam weight: HIGH, and easy to underestimate. This chapter's content is split across TWO weighted sections: Section II (premium payment/modes, beneficiary designations, settlement options — part of 15 questions) and Section IV (life settlements + tax treatment of premiums, proceeds and dividends, MECs — part of 8 questions). Beneficiary distribution and taxation are near-guaranteed points.

1. Premium Calculation ⭐

  • The THREE factors (memorize): Mortality, Interest, Expenses (loading).
  • Mortality has the greatest impact; comes from the CSO (Commissioners Standard Ordinary) Mortality Table = probability of death by age, expressed as deaths per thousand.
  • Interest = the insurer's investment return. A higher assumed interest rate LOWERS the premium.
  • Expenses (loading) = operating costs, commissions, administration.
  • Premiums are always quoted per $1,000 of coverage.
  • Other factors: age, sex (women pay less, longer life expectancy), health history, occupation, hobbies, habits (tobacco, DUI).

Premium types

  • Net (single) premium = mortality + interest (no expenses).
  • Net level annual premium = amount needed annually to fund future benefits.
  • Gross premium = net premium + expenses (what you actually pay).
  • Mortality rate = frequency of death; morbidity rate = frequency of disease/sickness.
  • Premium mode: annual is cheapest; monthly is the most expensive (more admin cost + insurer loses investment time). More frequent = higher total cost.

2. Premium Funding Methods

  • Single premium — one lump sum.
  • Fixed/level — spread evenly; overpay early to offset later costs.
  • Modified — lower initial premium, then ONE increase to a constant higher amount.
  • Graded — lower premiums that increase annually for a period, then level off.
  • Flexible — owner adjusts payments (universal life).
  • Minimum deposit (financed) insurance — uses policy loans to pay premiums; best for high tax brackets. IRS rule: at least 4 of the first 7 premiums must come from non-loan funds or it becomes a MEC.
  • Earned vs. unearned premium: earned = coverage already provided; unearned = refunded if cancelled. Reserves = funds set aside to pay future claims (proves solvency); legal reserve is set by the commissioner using the CSO table.

3. Cost Comparison Methods

  • Interest-adjusted net cost method — considers premiums, death benefits, cash value, and dividends.
  • Surrender cost index — average cost per $1,000 assuming you surrender for cash value. Best if cash value growth matters.
  • Net payment cost index — assumes you do NOT surrender (cash value omitted). Best if the death benefit is the priority.
  • Comparative interest rate method — the return an investment would need to match a cash value policy = the "buy term and invest the rest" comparison.

5. The FIVE Settlement Options ⭐⭐ (memorize)

  • Lump sum (cash) — single payment; the default and most common; income tax-free.
  • Interest only — insurer holds the proceeds and pays only interest (interest is taxable).
  • Fixed period — equal installments over a set number of years. (Time is fixed; the payment amount varies.)
  • Fixed amount — specified installment amount until principal + interest run out. (Amount is fixed; the duration varies.)
  • Life income — guaranteed payments for the beneficiary's lifetime (funded by a SPIA). Sub-options:
    • Straight / pure / single life income — HIGHEST payment, but nothing paid after death (greatest risk of forfeiture).
    • Life income with period certain — lifetime income; if the beneficiary dies within the certain period, a secondary payee finishes it out. Longer period certain = lower payment.
    • Refund life income — guarantees the full death benefit is paid out (installment refund = continued payments; cash refund = lump sum of the remainder).
    • Joint and survivor — covers 2+ people for life; often reduces after the first death.
EXAM TIP!Death proceeds are not income taxable, but any interest earned is taxable in the year earned, even if not paid out.
  • The policy owner may select a settlement option (and then the beneficiary cannot change it). Otherwise the beneficiary chooses at the time of death.
  • Spendthrift clause — protects proceeds from the beneficiary's creditors while held by the insurer. Beneficiary cannot commute (take present value in a lump sum) or encumber (use as loan collateral). No effect once paid as a lump sum.

6. Beneficiaries ⭐⭐

  • Primary → Secondary/Contingent → Tertiary. Contingent gets paid only if ALL primaries predecease the insured. No limit on depth of succession.
  • Revocable = change anytime, no notice to the beneficiary. Irrevocable = needs the beneficiary's written consent to change, assign, borrow, or surrender. (Owner may still take dividends and choose premium mode; irrevocable beneficiary may request a copy of the policy.)
  • Changing a beneficiary: Filing/recording method (predominant — effective the date the request is submitted) vs. Endorsement method (policy returned; effective when an officer approves and signs).
  • Beneficiary types: individual, minor, trust, estate, charity, business, class designation (e.g., "my children").

Per capita vs. per stirpes ⭐

  • Per capita = "per head." Split evenly among living named beneficiaries. This is the DEFAULT unless per stirpes is requested.
  • Per stirpes = "by bloodline/branch." A deceased beneficiary's share passes to their heirs. (Makes naming contingents less necessary.)

Special distributions

  • To an estate — happens if no beneficiary survives; exposed to probate, estate taxes, and creditors. Avoid.
  • To a minor — insurers generally will NOT pay a minor directly (no legal capacity); needs a guardian or trustee.
  • To a trust — the best choice for leaving proceeds to a minor (trustee manages); trust fees may reduce proceeds. Testamentary trust = created at death via will; inter vivos (living) trust = created during life.
  • Facility of payment provision — lets the insurer pay someone equitably entitled (e.g., who paid final expenses), typically if no claim is filed within 2 months.

Simultaneous death ⭐

  • Uniform Simultaneous Death Act — if the order of death cannot be determined, the INSURED is presumed to have died LAST (beneficiary presumed to die first), so proceeds go to the contingent beneficiary.
  • Common disaster provision — adds a survivorship clause: the primary beneficiary must outlive the insured by a stated period (typically 14-30 days) to collect. This protects the contingent beneficiary even when the order of death IS known.
  • Both exist to keep proceeds out of the primary beneficiary's estate (avoiding probate, death taxes, creditors).

7. Taxation ⭐⭐ (guaranteed points)

Premiums

  • Individual life premiums are a personal expense = NOT tax-deductible.
  • Deductible exceptions: policy owned by a qualified charity, court-ordered alimony, or premiums paid by an employer as an employee benefit.

Death proceeds

  • Lump-sum death benefit = INCOME TAX-FREE. Exception: the transfer-for-value rule (policy sold to another party before death).
  • The policy's value IS included in the owner's estate for estate tax purposes.
  • Paid in installments: principal is tax-free, interest is taxable.

While the insured is alive

  • Cash value grows tax-deferred. Cost basis = premiums paid minus dividends received.
  • Cost recovery rule: on surrender, only the amount exceeding the cost basis is taxable as ordinary income. If the cash value stays in the policy, it's never taxed.
  • Policy loans: generally no tax consequences (unless a MEC). Loan interest is not deductible.
  • Accelerated death benefits: tax-free if terminally ill (death expected within 2 years).
  • Dividends: tax-exempt (return of premium). But interest earned on accumulated dividends IS taxable.
  • MEC taxation: taxed LIFO (gain/earnings first) on any distribution, loan, or withdrawal, plus a 10% penalty before age 59½.

1035 Exchanges (tax-free swaps)

  • Life → life, endowment, or annuity ✅
  • Endowment → endowment or annuity ✅
  • Annuity → annuity ✅
  • Annuity → life insurance ❌ NOT allowed (would convert tax-deferred into tax-free).
  • Owner must receive no cash; complete within 60 days; cost basis carries over.

1. Agent Responsibility & Solicitation

  • Agent = field underwriter; acts in a fiduciary capacity. An unintentional mistake by a producer = a tort called an error and omission (hence E&O insurance).
  • Proper solicitation = only pursue business that fits the insurer's underwriting guidelines.

2. Insurable Interest (recap + new detail)

  • Required only at application/inception, not at time of loss (life/health).
  • Automatic: self, spouse, parent/child (both directions), business & its officers/directors/key employees, business partners, creditor/debtor (limited to the debt).
  • Amount of insurable interest is generally unlimited on yourself; a creditor-beneficiary is limited to the debt + interest. Insurers may also cap a child's coverage relative to a parent's own coverage amount.
  • Cannot buy insurance on another person without their consent (with narrow exceptions like insuring a minor).

3. Disclosures Before the First Premium ⭐

  • Buyer's Guide — generic publication explaining insurance types in general (a compliance document, not product-specific).
  • Policy Summary — describes the specific product: premiums, dividends, benefits, cash values, loan interest, cost indexes.
  • Both must be delivered BEFORE the agent collects the first premium.
EXAM TIP!Failure to provide disclosures before collecting the first premium = regulatory non-compliance. Frequently tested.

4. Purpose of Underwriting ⭐

  • Underwriting (= risk selection) protects the insurer from adverse selection.
  • Elements of insurable risk: significant enough to cause hardship, not catastrophic, premium must be reasonable, loss must be unpredictable/fortuitous, and the law of large numbers must apply.
  • Two underwriting questions: (1) Does insurable interest exist? (2) Is the applicant insurable?

Parties involved

Applicant (requests insurance) · Proposed insured (whose life is covered) · Policy owner (holds all rights, usually also the payor) · Payor (pays premiums) · Producer/agent (field underwriter) · Underwriter (classifies risk, sets rate).

EXAM TIP!Assume policy owner = payor = applicant = proposed insured are all the SAME PERSON unless the question says otherwise.

Underwriting phases

  • Pre-selection (by the agent): get complete/honest answers; cannot legally guarantee or bind coverage.
  • Post-selection (by the underwriting dept.): evaluates all sources, classifies as preferred / standard / substandard.

HIPAA privacy notice ⭐

1. How Group Life Works ⭐

  • Covers multiple people under ONE contract — typically employer-employee. Almost always annual renewable term.
  • Employer/sponsor = policy owner, receives the master policy. Employees get a certificate of insurance (not their own policy) and are called certificate holders.

  • Focuses on the group's characteristics, not each individual; leverages the law of large numbers.
  • Group life CANNOT exclude an employee for a physical impairment.
  • Persistency = % of policies that stay in force / don't lapse or get replaced — insurers track this since acquiring new group business is costly.

5. Conversion Privilege ⭐⭐ (guaranteed points)

  • On termination (voluntary or involuntary), the employee has 31 days to convert to an individual PERMANENT (whole life) policy, no proof of insurability required.
  • Coverage continues under the group policy during those 31 days — if death occurs before conversion, the group policy pays.
  • Dependents may also convert without proof of insurability.
  • Premium is based on the attained (current) age at conversion (so it's usually higher than the group rate).
  • If the entire master policy terminates: members covered 5+ consecutive years may convert; the converted policy may be capped (e.g., $10,000 max); must apply within 31-60 days.

6. Other Group Forms

  • Group credit life — banks/lenders; pays off the loan balance if the borrower dies; usually decreasing term; premium based on claims experience, not age; borrower pays.
  • Blanket life — covers a group exposed to the same hazard (airline passengers, students); no individuals named, no certificates issued.
  • Retired Lives Reserve (RLR) — ART + a reserve account that funds premiums into retirement; employer contributions are tax-deductible, not taxable to the employee until used.

1. What an Annuity Is ⭐

  • Only life insurance companies can issue annuities.
  • Purpose: protect against outliving your assets (opposite of life insurance, which creates an estate; an annuity liquidates an estate systematically).
  • Accumulation phase (pay-in, tax-deferred growth) → Annuitization (payout; once annuitized, it's irreversible).
  • Risk-sharing contract: if the annuitant dies early, the insurer benefits; if the annuitant lives long, the annuitant benefits.
  • Regulated by the state (fixed) AND the SEC/FINRA (variable).

6. Classification by Number of Lives

  • Single/individual life — one annuitant, no survivorship, highest payment, most common.
  • Joint life — 2+ annuitants; ends at the FIRST death.
  • Joint and survivor — 2+ annuitants; continues until the LAST annuitant dies.

7. Surrender Charges, Non-Forfeiture, and Riders ⭐

  • Surrender charges ("back-end loads") — assessed on excess withdrawals (above a free amount, e.g. 10%/yr) or full surrender; typically decrease over time (e.g., 8% year 1, -1%/yr). Different from the 10% federal tax penalty — both can apply together.
  • Non-forfeiture value = fund value minus surrender charges. Before annuitization = premiums + interest - withdrawals/charges.
  • Riders: GMWB (Guaranteed Minimum Withdrawal Benefit — lets you withdraw a fixed % even in a downturn), GMIB (Guaranteed Minimum Income Benefit — guarantees a minimum payout at annuitization; must annuitize to use it), GMAB (Guaranteed Minimum Accumulation Benefit — guarantees a minimum accumulated value after a waiting period; doesn't require annuitizing).

8. Qualified vs. Non-Qualified ⭐

  • Non-qualified — funded with after-tax dollars; no premium deduction; growth still tax-deferred.
  • Qualified — part of a tax-qualified retirement plan (funds an IRA, TSA/403(b), 401(k), pension, etc.); premiums may be deductible; often funded via salary reduction (pre-tax).
  • Either way, interest/growth is ALWAYS tax-deferred.

9. Uses of Annuities

  • Individual: retirement income, IRA funding, education funds, structured settlements (lawsuit payouts), lottery winnings distribution.
  • Business: executive compensation, buy-sell funding, key person protection, corporate pensions, employee retention.
  • Group vs. individual annuities: group = employer-purchased, standardized, lower cost, often pre-tax, tied to employment. Individual = personally purchased, customizable, after-tax (unless qualified), portable.

10. Suitability ⭐

  • Producers must have reasonable grounds to believe a recommendation is suitable, based on age, income, financial situation/experience/objectives, intended use, time horizon, existing assets, liquidity needs, risk tolerance, tax status.
  • Senior consumer = age 65+ (or either party in a joint purchase). Extra suitability documentation required for seniors.

11. Taxation ⭐⭐

  • Exclusion ratio = tax-free portion of each payment. Formula: Investment in contract ÷ Expected return. (Example: $120,000 invested ÷ $150,000 expected return = 80% tax-free; on a $6,000 payment, $4,800 is tax-free, $1,200 is taxable.)
  • Early withdrawal (before age 59½): 10% penalty + ordinary income tax on the gain. No penalty if: disabled, age 59½+, deceased, it's an immediate annuity, or a qualified pension plan distribution.
  • LIFO taxation — withdrawals treated as earnings first (taxable) for contracts after Aug 14, 1982. (Contracts before that date use FIFO — principal out first.)
  • At death (before annuitization): beneficiary gets the greater of accumulated value or total premiums paid; the gain over premiums is taxable as ordinary income. Beneficiary can pick installment/life-income within 60 days of death to spread the tax hit.
  • 1035 exchange: annuity → annuity = tax-free. Life insurance → annuity = tax-free. Annuity → life insurance = NOT allowed tax-free.
  • Corporate-owned annuities must name a natural person as the annuitant ("measurable life") to keep tax deferral; if a non-natural entity (the corporation itself) is the annuitant, interest is taxed annually as earned. Exceptions: held by a trust/agent for a natural person, acquired by an estate, held under a qualified plan (TSA/IRA), or a single-premium immediate annuity.

1. Determining the Proper Amount ⭐

  • Human Life Value (HLV) approach — capitalizes a person's future net earnings. Formula concept: (income - expenses) × years until retirement, discounted to present value. Only measures lost income — ignores family's specific needs/goals.
  • Needs approach — based on the family's actual financial goals: final expenses, debt payoff, education, emergency fund, survivor income. Formula: Total capital needs - liquid assets = insurance needed. Must account for Social Security and the blackout period (gap in benefits from insured's death until surviving spouse's retirement eligibility; children get benefits until age 18, or 19 and 2 months if still a full-time high school student, or indefinitely if disabled).
  • Other approaches (know the names, not the math): Multiple earnings method (e.g., 5x salary), Interest-only method (keep principal intact, live off interest), Single needs method (address one specific need), Capital needs analysis (immediate cash needs: final expenses, taxes, debt, education), Seat-of-the-pants method (arbitrary guess — NOT a real methodology, just picking a number).
EXAM TIP!HLV = income replacement ONLY. Needs approach = specific family goals AND accounts for Social Security/blackout period. You don't need to memorize the math, just know what each approach considers.

2. Personal Uses ⭐

  • Life insurance is a bought-and-sold asset; insurable interest is required only at purchase, doesn't need to persist (a policy stays valid even after divorce, as we covered in Ch 3).
  • Lump-sum needs: final expenses, debt repayment, mortgage payoff, estate protection, charitable giving.
  • Income needs: survivor income, education funding, retirement supplement, cash accumulation/liquidity.
  • The policy is an asset included in the owner's estate (taxable if the estate exceeds the federal exemption — $13.99 million per individual in 2025).

3. Business Uses: Buy-Sell Agreements ⭐⭐ (the guaranteed-testable part)

  • A buy-sell agreement guarantees cash is available to buy a deceased owner's business interest, funded by life insurance.
  • Sole proprietor: two-step plan — a buy-sell agreement naming a successor (often a key employee), funded by a policy the employee owns on the proprietor's life.
  • Partnership: a partner's death legally dissolves the partnership unless a buy-sell agreement exists. Two structures:
    • Entity plan — the PARTNERSHIP/business buys, owns, and pays for a policy on each partner; business is beneficiary; uses proceeds to buy the deceased's share. Fewer policies needed with many partners (just N policies for N partners).
    • Cross-purchase plan — EACH PARTNER individually buys a policy on every other partner; partners are beneficiaries; they personally use proceeds to buy the deceased's share. Better for a SMALL number of partners — policies needed = n × (n-1) (with 3 partners, that's 6 policies).
  • Closely held corporation: entity version = stock redemption plan (corporation buys stock back from the deceased's estate). A 303 redemption plan applies when stock is 35%+ of the deceased's adjusted gross estate (helps cover estate tax without a forced sale).
EXAM TIP!Entity plan = the BUSINESS owns the policies (simpler, fewer policies, good for many owners). Cross-purchase = the OWNERS individually own policies on each other (n×(n-1) policies, better for few owners).

4. Corporate-Owned Life Insurance (COLI) & Key Person

  • COLI — company owns a policy on a key employee; company is beneficiary; premiums NOT deductible; proceeds tax-free. A "change of insured" provision lets the employer swap in a replacement employee without writing a new policy.
  • Key employee (key person) insurance — compensates the business for losing someone with critical skills/knowledge. Third-party ownership: the business is applicant, owner, payor, AND beneficiary; the employee is just the insured.
  • Four purposes: (1) business indemnification, (2) reserve fund (cash value = living benefit/balance-sheet asset), (3) business credit (aids loan approval/repayment guarantee), (4) favorable tax treatment.
  • Premiums NOT deductible; death proceeds tax-free to the business. Proceeds are NOT included in the employee's estate (assuming no ownership interest).
  • Commonly funded with whole life or universal life; term for short-term needs.

CH 11Retirement Plans▼
part of 8 Q · light
Exam weight: LIGHT. Maps to TN outline Section IV.D (Qualified vs. non-qualified plans) — shares the same 8-question bucket as Ch 8, 10, 12. Learn the qualified-plan types, IRA rules, and the key numbers; skip the deep math.

1. Qualified vs. Non-Qualified ⭐

  • Qualified plans — meet federal (IRS/ERISA) requirements, get favorable tax treatment. Employer contributions = deductible business expense. Employee isn't taxed on contributions or growth until withdrawal (usually retirement, lower tax bracket).
  • Non-qualified plans — don't meet federal requirements, no special tax treatment. Used to reward highly compensated/key employees since they're allowed to discriminate in who's covered. Employer contributions are NOT deductible until the employee actually receives them. Can be funded (assets held in trust/escrow) or unfunded (employee just relies on the employer's unsecured promise).

2. ERISA (Qualified Employer Plans) ⭐

Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974 — protects employee rights under employer-sponsored plans. To be "tax-qualified":

  • Must be a formal, written document, communicated to all employees.
  • Must be for employees' benefit only, intended to be ongoing.
  • Minimum age 21 + 1 year of service to enroll (if 100% vesting on entry, up to 2 years of service may be required).
  • Cannot discriminate in favor of highly compensated employees.
  • "Top heavy" = key employees hold more than 60% of total plan value → must make minimum contributions for non-key employees.
  • Must provide survivor benefits + meet minimum vesting standards; plan assets legally segregated from the employer's own assets.
  • New employees get a Summary Plan Description within 90 days. Form 5500 = annual ERISA disclosure filing.
  • Exempt from ERISA: church, governmental, and collectively bargained plans.

Vesting schedules

  • Full vesting after 5 years, OR 20% after 3 years, reaching 100% by year 7 (graded).
  • Employees are ALWAYS 100% vested in their own contributions, instantly.

3. Defined Contribution Plans ⭐

Contribution amount is defined/known; the eventual BENEFIT is NOT (depends on investment performance). Employee bears the investment risk. Contributions grow tax-deferred; employee contributions are always fully vested.

Money purchase plan
Key featureEmployer MUST contribute a fixed % of salary every year (not tied to profit)
Profit-sharing plan
Key featureContribution tied to company profits; NOT required every year; must be "recurring and substantial" for tax benefits
Stock bonus plan
Key featureBenefits paid in company stock, NOT based on profits
ESOP (Employee Stock Ownership Plan)

EXAM TIP!Money purchase = employer MUST contribute a fixed %, profits or not. Profit-sharing = only if there ARE profits, and not required every year.

4. Defined Benefit Plans ⭐

  • The BENEFIT is defined/guaranteed (usually via a formula based on salary + years of service); the employer bears the investment risk.
  • "Pension" typically = a defined benefit plan.
  • Must provide definitely determinable benefits and systematic payments over time (usually for life).
  • Formula example: 1.5% × years of service × final average salary = annual pension for life.
EXAM TIP!Defined CONTRIBUTION = contribution known, benefit varies (employee's risk). Defined BENEFIT = benefit known/guaranteed, employer bears the risk.

5. Salary Reduction (Deferral) Plans ⭐

  • 401(k) — private-sector employees defer part of their salary pre-tax; often with employer match; grows tax-deferred. Catch-up contributions allowed at age 50+.
  • 403(b) / Tax-Sheltered Annuity (TSA) — ONLY for employees of nonprofit, educational, religious, 501(c)(3) organizations (including public school teachers). Contributions excluded from current taxable income.
  • Section 457 — for state/local government and nonprofit employees; deferred amounts excluded from gross income until received; similar contribution limits to 401(k).

6. Plans for Small Employers / Self-Employed ⭐

  • Keogh (HR-10) — for unincorporated businesses/self-employed; can be defined contribution OR defined benefit; same limits/rules as corporate plans (thanks to the 1962 Self-Employed Individuals Retirement Act).
  • SEP (Simplified Employee Pension) — employer contributes directly to each employee's own IRA; simpler/cheaper than a full qualified plan; allows much higher contributions than a regular IRA; cannot discriminate toward highly compensated employees.
  • SIMPLE plan — for businesses with ≤100 employees (each earning ≥$5,000 prior year); employer cannot have another qualified plan; can be structured as an IRA or 401(k); all contributions immediately/fully vested; lower admin costs, no ERISA filing burden; catch-up contributions allowed at 50+.

7. Individual Retirement Accounts (IRAs) ⭐⭐

Traditional IRA

  • Anyone with earned income (any age) may contribute up to the annual limit ($7,500) or 100% of compensation, whichever is less. Catch-up (age 50+): additional $1,100 (total $8,600).
  • Deductibility depends on: (1) whether covered by an employer plan, (2) income level. NOT covered by an employer plan → full deduction regardless of income. Covered by an employer plan → deduction phases out with higher income.
  • Contributing ≠ deducting — anyone with earned income CAN contribute; whether it's deductible depends on the factors above.
  • RMDs (Required Minimum Distributions) must begin by April 1 of the year after turning 73 (SECURE Act 2.0). Missing an RMD → 25% excise tax on the shortfall (reduced to 10% if corrected within 2 years).
  • Early withdrawal (before 59½): 10% penalty + income tax, EXCEPT: death/disability, qualifying medical expenses, higher education, first-time home purchase, health insurance premiums while unemployed, correcting excess contributions.
  • Distributions taxed like annuities: nondeductible-contribution portion = tax-free; interest/deductible portion = taxable.

Roth IRA

  • Contributions are after-tax (nondeductible); qualified withdrawals (including earnings) are completely tax-free.
  • No age limit to contribute (unlike... well, there's no upper age cutoff), BUT subject to INCOME limits — high earners may be phased out entirely.
  • NO Required Minimum Distributions during the owner's lifetime — funds can stay and even pass to heirs.
  • Qualified withdrawal requires BOTH: (1) held 5+ years, AND (2) a qualifying reason (age 59½, death, disability, or first-time home purchase).
  • Non-qualified withdrawal — earnings portion is taxable, and if under 59½, also hit with the 10% penalty. (Contributions themselves always come out tax/penalty-free, since already taxed.)

Spousal IRA

A working spouse may fund a separate IRA for a non-working spouse, up to the same annual limit ($7,500, or $8,600 if 50+), even if the working spouse is in an employer plan.

Rollover IRA

  • Must be reinvested within 60 days; participant must not take physical possession of the funds, or a mandatory 20% withholding applies (even if redeposited within 60 days).
  • Direct (trustee-to-trustee) transfer avoids the 20% withholding entirely.
  • One-rollover-per-year rule (aggregated across all IRAs owned) — EXCEPT: Roth conversions, trustee-to-trustee transfers, and IRA-to-plan/plan-to-IRA/plan-to-plan rollovers.
  • Cannot roll over: Required Minimum Distributions, or excess-contribution distributions.
  • Surviving spouse exception — a spouse who inherits IRA/plan benefits can roll them into an IRA in their own name (assets to a spouse are generally estate-tax-free via the unlimited marital deduction).
EXAM TIP!Traditional = deduct now, pay tax later, RMDs at 73. Roth = pay tax now (after-tax contributions), tax-free later, NO RMDs ever.

8. Educational Savings Plans

  • Coverdell ESA ("Education IRA") — for primary/secondary/higher-ed expenses; unused funds can roll to another beneficiary before age 30.
  • Section 529 plan — state-run; college savings plan (use at any college) or prepaid tuition plan (locks in current in-state tuition rates). Earnings + qualified withdrawals are federal tax-free.

1. Purpose & Basics ⭐

  • OASDI = Old-Age, Survivors, and Disability Insurance = the official name for Social Security. Created by the Social Security Act (1935).
  • Provides retirement income, disability income, a lump-sum death benefit, and survivor income.
  • Social Security supplements, NOT replaces, personal financial/insurance planning.

  • ~25% of state/local government employees with their own pension who opted out.
  • Railroad workers (separate Railroad Retirement program).

4. Calculating Benefits ⭐

3-step process:

  • AIME (Average Indexed Monthly Earnings) — based on the highest 35 years of earnings, inflation-indexed.
  • PIA (Primary Insurance Amount) — the base benefit amount at full retirement age (FRA); ALL Social Security benefit types are based on the PIA.
  • Adjustment based on claiming age.

EXAM TIP!Memorize: 70% at 62, 100% at 67 (FRA), 124% at 70.

Earnings test (only applies BEFORE FRA)

  • Below FRA all year: benefit reduced $1 for every $2 earned above the annual limit.
  • Year you REACH FRA: reduced $1 for every $3 earned above a (different, higher) limit — only counting earnings before the month you hit FRA.
  • At or after FRA: NO earnings limit at all — work as much as you want, no reduction.

Dual benefit liability

If eligible for two benefits (e.g., your own retirement AND a deceased spouse's benefit), you can only collect ONE — the greater of the two.

5. Disability Benefits ⭐⭐ (guaranteed points)

  • Must be fully insured (+ the 20/40 rule if 31+).
  • 5-month waiting period — benefits begin accruing in month 6, first check arrives after that.
  • Definition of disability: unable to do ANY substantial gainful work in the national economy (not just your old job); condition must be expected to last 12+ months OR result in death. This is total disability, not partial.
EXAM TIP!Disability = fully insured + 20/40 rule + 5-month wait + TOTAL disability + 12-month/death duration. All frequently tested together.

6. Survivor / Death Benefits ⭐⭐ (guaranteed points)

  • Lump-sum death benefit = $255. Paid ONLY to: a surviving spouse who was living with the deceased, OR a spouse/child already eligible for monthly benefits.
  • Full survivor benefits require the worker to be fully insured; limited survivor benefits only need currently insured status.

Benefit amounts by beneficiary

Spouse at 60~71.5% of PIA (earliest, reduced)

Maximum family benefit — caps the TOTAL paid to a family; individual payments get reduced proportionally when multiple beneficiaries qualify on the same record.

EXAM TIP!$255 lump-sum death benefit; spouse gets 100% of PIA at FRA or ~71.5% at age 60; kids get 75% each (to 18, or 19 if in HS, or indefinitely if disabled before 22).

Blackout period ⭐⭐ (guaranteed points)

  • STARTS: when the youngest child turns 16.
  • ENDS: when the surviving spouse turns 60 (eligible for their own reduced widow(er)'s benefit).
  • During the blackout, the surviving spouse gets NO benefit (unless caring for a child under 16, which is what ends it at 16). Children's own benefits are unaffected and continue to age 18 (19 if still in high school).
EXAM TIP!Blackout = starts at child's age 16, ends at surviving spouse's age 60. This is one of the most frequently tested facts in the whole chapter.

1. Powers of the Commissioner ⭐⭐

  • Commissioner of Commerce and Insurance = chief officer of the TN Dept. of Commerce & Insurance; appointed by the Governor for a 4-year term.
  • The Commissioner enforces laws — does NOT make laws, propose legislation, or prosecute violators (refers criminal matters to the attorney general/district attorney) and cannot impose a prison sentence.
  • Powers: issue licenses & certificates of authority; approve policy forms; review rates (TN is a "file and use" state — rates are effective once filed unless the Commissioner rejects them; rates can't be excessive, inadequate, or unfairly discriminatory).
  • Hearings: for unfair competition/deceptive acts, unlicensed business, or whenever the public interest is served.
  • Investigations [56-6-120]: accused gets a copy of the complaint within 30 days; witnesses get 14 days' written notice; investigations must complete within 2 years; Commissioner notifies both parties within 30 days of completion.
  • Regulatory authority [56-6-107 to 112]: applies even if the person's license has lapsed or been surrendered. Audits authorized insurers at least every 5 years (insurer pays the exam costs).
  • Penalties [56-6-112(e)]: up to $1,000/violation (max $100,000 total); if knowing, up to $25,000/violation (max $250,000 total). Civil penalty becomes final 30 days after the order is served; violator has 30 days to appeal.
EXAM TIP!Commissioner = appointed by Governor, 4-year term, enforces (doesn't make) law, can't jail anyone. Penalties: $1,000/$100k standard; $25,000/$250k if knowing.

2. Key Definitions ⭐⭐

  • Insurance producer = anyone who sells/solicits/negotiates insurance for compensation. Must be licensed AND appointed before selling.
  • Business entity = corp/association/partnership/LLC/LLP/etc.; can be licensed as a producer if it designates a licensed employee for compliance.
  • Limited lines producer = authorized for limited lines only (travel insurance, credit life, mortgage guaranty, personal property, crop hail, title insurance).
  • Authorized/Admitted insurer = has a Certificate of Authority. Unauthorized/Nonadmitted = does not (producer risks being liable for unpaid claims if they sell for one).
  • Fiduciary = producers hold client funds in trust; cannot commingle with personal funds.
  • Domestic = incorporated in TN. Foreign = incorporated in another U.S. state. Alien = incorporated in another country. (All still need a Certificate of Authority to transact in TN regardless of domicile.)

3. License Requirements ⭐⭐

  • Resident producer applicant must: be 18+; not have committed a disqualifying act; pass the state exam; submit application + fees; not intend to primarily write controlled business.
  • Passing score for ANY TN insurance exam = 70%.
  • Retake rule: fail once → wait 10 days; each subsequent fail → wait 30 days. Fail to pass within 6 months of the first attempt → must retake pre-licensing education.
  • Fees: $50 application (producer or limited lines); $60 producer license renewal; $30 limited lines renewal; $15 appointment/termination fee.
  • Producer appointments [56-6-115]: insurer must notify Commissioner within 15 days of appointment/first application. Commissioner verifies eligibility within 30 days; if ineligible, notifies insurer within 5 days.
  • Appointment termination [56-6-117]: insurer notifies Commissioner within 30 days; insurer mails a copy to the terminated agent within 15 days of that notice; agent then has 30 days to submit written comments.
  • Nonresident licensing: needs a reciprocal agreement; no TN exam required if licensed/in good standing in home state.
  • Moving TO Tennessee: apply to convert nonresident → resident license (no exam/pre-licensing redo) within 90 days of the move.
  • Change of address/name: report within 30 days of the change (no fee/application required).
  • Temporary license [56-6-111]: up to 180 days, no exam required (e.g., for a producer entering active military service, or a surviving spouse/next of kin of a deceased/disabled producer). NOT renewable.
  • License renewal [56-6-107]: valid for 24 months from the last day of the licensee's birth month. Reinstatement within 12 months of lapse = no re-exam, but pay double the unpaid renewal fee.
  • Continuing Education: 24 hours every 2 years, 3 hours must be ethics. Exempt: continuously licensed since Jan 1, 1994; business entities; nonresidents meeting home-state CE. Up to 12 hours may carry over to the next cycle (ethics hours may NOT carry over). Annuity producers: one-time 4-hour training course.
  • License exceptions (no license needed): salaried insurer officers/employees; group plan administrators receiving no commission; those giving only technical advice; salaried creditor employees enrolling debtors in group credit life/health; fraternal benefit society members (uncompensated); auto rental employees selling optional coverage for ≤90 days.
EXAM TIP!Passing score = 70%. CE = 24 hrs/2 yrs (3 ethics), up to 12 hrs carry over (not ethics). Appointment notice = 15 days. Reinstate within 12 months, double fee, no re-exam. Temporary license = max 180 days.

4. License Suspension/Revocation ⭐

  • Commissioner may probate, suspend, revoke, refuse to renew, or deny for (among others): false info on application; violating insurance laws/orders; fraud to obtain a license; misappropriating client money; misrepresenting contract terms; felony conviction; unfair trade practices; license action in another state; forgery; cheating on the licensing exam; knowingly transacting for an unauthorized insurer; failing to pay child support/alimony; failing to pay state income tax.
  • Monetary fine: $100 to $1,000 per violation, in addition to any license action. Applies even if the license has lapsed/been surrendered. Commissioner may notify the NAIC.
  • Notice: Commissioner must give written notice within 30 days of a license denial; must give notice before a suspension/revocation hearing.

5. Unfair Trade Practices ⭐⭐ (memorize each)

  • False advertising — false/misleading statements about coverage or an insurer's financial condition; ads must identify the insurer; insurer is responsible for testimonial accuracy.
  • Defamation — false/unsubstantiated public statements maligning a competitor's financial condition.
  • Boycotting/coercion/intimidation — e.g., a lender requiring you to buy insurance from a specific company/agency as a loan condition.
  • Unfair discrimination — different treatment based on marital status, race, color, national origin, creed, ancestry (without a business/legal reason). NOT unfair: rate differences by age/gender/risk profile (mortality-based), or by tobacco use — that's legal/fair discrimination.
  • Rebating — giving a prospect something of value NOT in the contract to induce a sale (kickback of commission, paying their premium, favorable backdating, free services/employment). NOT rebating: dividends/surplus reductions on nonparticipating policies, accepting a bona fide premium-finance obligation, class-based premium reductions, industrial-debit collection-expense savings, group experience-based rate adjustments.
  • Unfair claims settlement practices — misrepresenting policy facts; not responding promptly; no reasonable claims-investigation standards; not affirming/denying coverage promptly after proof of loss; not attempting good-faith settlement when liability is clear; delaying settlement just because other insurance exists (claimant chooses which to recover from); forcing insureds to sue by lowballing; failing to pay workers' comp as required.
  • Misrepresentation — misrepresenting any policy fact (terms, benefits, value, cost, effective date, existence of a contract).
  • Twisting — misrepresentations to induce someone to drop/borrow against an existing policy to buy a new one.
  • Financial planner rule: a producer can't call themselves a "financial planner/investment adviser/consultant" etc. unless properly certified, and if doing financial planning, must disclose they're also an insurance salesperson earning commission.
  • Referral fees: the referrer cannot discuss policy terms; payment cannot depend on a sale; if the referrer isn't employed/affiliated with the producer, max fee = $25.
EXAM TIP!Rebating = giving something NOT in the contract to induce a sale. Twisting = inducing someone to REPLACE an existing policy through misrepresentation. Boycotting = coercing someone into buying from a specific insurer.

7. Dividends & Cost-Index Definitions

  • Dividends = a refund of premium, therefore tax-free. Participating policies must offer dividend options (cash, reduce premium, paid-up insurance, accumulate at interest) by end of year 3.
  • Cash dividend = current dividend applied to the gross premium.
  • Equivalent level annual dividend = total 10- or 20-year dividends ÷ years = an average.
  • Equivalent level death benefit = pays a guaranteed amount regardless of cause of death.
  • Surrender cost index — best for buyers who prioritize cash value growth and plan to surrender in 10-20 yrs.
  • Net payment cost index — best for buyers focused on death protection, not cash value.
  • Buyer's Guide = state-prescribed generic wording; explains policy types + helps compare via cost index.
  • Policy Summary = specific to the actual policy being sold; must be titled "STATEMENT OF POLICY COST AND BENEFIT INFORMATION"; must include insurer name/address, generic policy/rider names, policy loan rate, 10- and 20-year cost indexes, dividend disclaimer (if applicable), guaranteed values through year 5+ (and age 60-65), and the date prepared.

8. Disclosure Requirements ⭐⭐

  • Buyer's Guide + Policy Summary must be given BEFORE accepting the first premium — UNLESS the policy has an unconditional free-look of at least 10 days, in which case they may instead be delivered at policy delivery.
  • Must also be given to any prospective purchaser upon request.
  • Document retention: insurer keeps a copy of every authorized sales form on file for 3 years after its last use.
  • Producer must identify themselves as a life insurance producer (and name the insurer) before a sales presentation. Direct-mail insurers must fully identify themselves too.
  • Prohibited terms: "financial planner," "investment advisor," "financial consultant," etc. can't be used to imply non-commission-based advisory work unless true.
  • Time value of money: cost comparisons must account for it (or disclose that they don't).
  • Presentation of benefits: guaranteed and non-guaranteed benefits cannot be shown as one combined number — must be shown separately, in proximity.
EXAM TIP!Buyer's Guide + Policy Summary = before 1st premium, UNLESS a 10-day+ free-look exists (then can wait until delivery).

9. Replacement Rules ⭐⭐

  • Exempt from replacement rules: group life, credit life, mass-merchandised policies, tax-qualified retirement plan policies, and small policies (< $500 cash value AND < $5,000 face amount).
  • Producer's duties: present a signed Notice Regarding Replacement (both applicant and producer sign; copy left with applicant); get a list of all existing policies being replaced; leave the applicant copies of sales materials used; submit a copy of the replacement notice with the application to the new insurer.
  • New producers (licensed < 120 days): can't take replacement applications until 10 days after mailing the notice of intent to replace to the existing insurer.
  • Notice of replacement: producer gives a written comparison/summary at the policyholder's request, following the appointing insurer's instructions.
EXAM TIP!Replacement exemption threshold: under $500 cash value AND under $5,000 face amount. New producers (<120 days) wait 10 days before taking replacement apps.

10. Annuity Suitability & Education ⭐

  • Producer must have reasonable grounds the recommendation is suitable, based on: age, income, financial situation/needs/experience/objectives, intended use, time horizon, existing assets, liquidity needs, net worth, risk tolerance, tax status, present income.
  • Insurers must maintain a supervision system: inform producers of requirements, set product-training standards, provide product-specific training materials, and review each annuity recommendation before issuance.
  • Annuity training: one-time 4-hour course (approved by the Dept.), covering annuity types/parties, contract provisions, taxation, uses, and proper sales/replacement/disclosure practices.
  • Records retention: suitability info kept 5 years after the transaction. Insurer MAY (not required to) maintain this on the producer's behalf.
