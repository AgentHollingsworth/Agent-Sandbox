STARTHow to Use This▼
read first
Don't try to memorize the script. Learn the logic, and the words come.

The script is about 3,000 words. Nobody memorizes that cold. What good agents actually know is the structure — what each part of the call is for, what has to happen before the next thing can happen, and roughly a dozen lines that carry real weight. Everything else you can say in your own words once you understand why it's there.

This guide gives you that structure. Work through it with the Fresh Lead Script open beside it.

STEP 1Four Phases, Not Ten Stages▼
memorize first
🧠 WHY THIS MATTERSTen stages is too many to hold in your head under pressure. Four is easy. Every stage belongs to one of four phases, and each phase has a single job.
1. OPEN
Stages1–3
The one jobEarn the right to ask them questions
2. LEARN
Stages4–5
The one jobFind out what they need and what they qualify for
3. SHOW
Stages6–8
The one jobPresent the options and get them to choose one
4. CLOSE
Stages9–10
The one jobDo the paperwork and lock it in so it sticks
DO THISMemorize this first: OPEN · LEARN · SHOW · CLOSE. If you're ever lost on a call, ask yourself which phase you're in and what its job is. That alone will get you back on track.

SELF-TEST

  • Say the four phases and their jobs out loud, without looking. Then name which stages live in each. Do this until it's boring.

PHASE 1OPEN — Stages 1–3▼
earn the right
Job: earn the right to ask them questions. Nothing is being sold yet.
🧠 WHY THIS MATTERSThis phase exists because a stranger has no obligation to answer personal health questions over the phone. Everything in Stages 1–3 is buying permission for Stage 4. If you rush it, Stage 4 fights you.

Stage 1 — The Open

Confirm they're the right person and remind them this was their idea.

The move: you're not introducing a sales call, you're following up on their request. That framing is the whole stage.

⚓ ANCHOR LINE:"I wanted to quickly touch base with you about the request you made…"

Then the fork: have they already been contacted? Either way you land in the same place — one path acknowledges the other agent and probes what happened, the other just moves forward.

HOW PEOPLE BLOW ITSounding too polished. Slightly unsure and friendly reads as a human; confident and smooth reads as a telemarketer and gets you hung up on. Practice it too casual on purpose so you can hear the difference.

SELF-TEST

  • Run the open both ways — contacted and not contacted — without looking. Do they both land in Stage 2?

Stage 2 — Positioning

Establish authority, promise brevity, and flip the frame from selling to screening.

Three jobs in two sentences. "Manager" gives you standing. "4–5 quick questions" makes it feel small. And the qualify line flips the entire dynamic — you're not asking them to buy, you're checking whether they're even eligible.

⚓ ANCHOR LINE:"The biggest thing I can do throughout this process is just make sure you do actually qualify for the coverage."
🧠 WHY THIS MATTERSWhy this line matters more than any other early line: it makes the rest of the call a screening, not a pitch. You will reference it again in Stage 8 — that callback is what makes the close feel like a small step instead of a decision.
HOW PEOPLE BLOW ITSkipping it because it feels like filler. It isn't — it's the setup for your close forty minutes later.

SELF-TEST

  • Explain to someone else, in your own words, why the qualify line changes the whole call.

Stage 3 — Credentials Text

Kill the "is this a scam?" question before it's ever asked.

Sending your license and business card early does two things: it answers the trust question preemptively, and it gives you something to reference back to during the application.

HOW PEOPLE BLOW ITSkipping it to save time, then spending five minutes in Stage 9 defending yourself when they balk at the Social. The credentials text is cheap insurance.

Objection parked here: "Who do you work for?" Independent broker → name the A-rated carriers → pivot straight to pen and paper. Notice the answer ends on a question — that's deliberate, it hands control back to you. Don't stop after "private brokers" and wait for approval.

SELF-TEST

  • Deliver the "who do you work for" answer and land on the pen-and-paper question without pausing in the middle.
PHASE 2LEARN — Stages 4–5▼
biggest phase
Job: find out what they need, what they qualify for, and what you're competing against.

Stage 4 — The Seven Questions

Gather everything you'll need for the presentation and the application.

The seven split into three groups. Learn the groups, not the list:

Health (Q1–5)
QuestionsDOB · meds · major history · smoking · height/weight
What it tells youDo they qualify, and at what price
Motivation (Q6)
QuestionsWhat was your main goal?
What it tells youWhich product, and whether Stage 5 happens
Competition (Q7)
QuestionsExisting coverage
What it tells youWhat you're up against
🧠 WHY THIS MATTERSThis is also your exam material. Questions 1–5 are underwriting and risk classification — the same content as your course. If you understand why a carrier asks about A1C or insulin-vs-pills, you're studying both at once.
⚓ ANCHOR LINE (Q6):"When you originally filled this out, what was your main goal?"

Q6 is the most important question in the entire script. Their answer decides Final Expense vs. IUL/Term in Stage 7, and whether you run Stage 5 at all. Everything downstream branches from it.

HOW PEOPLE BLOW ITReciting the seven like a form instead of having a conversation. Real people answer out of order, ramble, and go off-topic. The questions are easy — holding the thread while someone tells you about their sister's surgery is the actual skill.

And: not writing it down. Stage 9 is built entirely on these notes. Re-asking sounds like you weren't listening.

SELF-TEST

  • Have a partner answer as a difficult client — vague, chatty, out of order. Get all seven without a script and without losing your place.

Stage 5 — Term vs. Permanent

Only runs if they're under 65 AND leaving money behind. Otherwise skip it.

Term ends and leaves them with nothing. Permanent lasts and locks the price. Then you ask which fits — and roll with whatever they pick.

HOW PEOPLE BLOW ITRunning it on the wrong client. A 72-year-old covering final expenses doesn't need this conversation, and having it wastes the call and muddies the presentation.

SELF-TEST

  • Given a client profile, decide in two seconds whether Stage 5 applies. Run five profiles.
PHASE 3SHOW — Stages 6–8▼
won or lost here
Job: present three options and get them to choose one. This is where the call is won or lost.

Stage 6 — Pen and Paper

Get them writing before any number leaves your mouth.

🧠 WHY THIS MATTERSSomeone holding a pen is engaged. The three options become numbers they own instead of noise on a phone call. This is a small stage doing heavy lifting — it's why the presentation lands.
HOW PEOPLE BLOW ITGiving prices before they have a pen. It's one of the five most common ways new agents lose calls. Wait for them.

Stage 7 — Present the Options

Explain living benefits, then walk Gold, Silver, Bronze.

Living benefits first, every time — even if they say they know what it means. It's the differentiator on every option you're about to name.

🥇 Gold
Final Expense$30,000
IUL / Term$100,000
Typical monthly$200 – $250
🥈 Silver
Final Expense$20,000
IUL / Term$75,000
Typical monthly$125 – $199
🥉 Bronze
Final Expense$15,000
IUL / Term$50,000
Typical monthly$60 – $124
🧠 WHY THIS MATTERSThe structure is doing something. Gold gets presented with "this isn't the option I typically recommend, but I'm required to show you all your options." That does two things: it makes you honest rather than pushy, and it makes Silver feel like their idea rather than your suggestion. Bronze is framed as locking in age and health now — a foot in the door, not a lesser choice.
HOW PEOPLE BLOW ITFumbling the numbers. Nothing costs credibility faster mid-presentation. Memorize the bands cold — you should be able to say all three tiers for both products without thinking.

SELF-TEST

  • Recite all six coverage amounts and all three price bands from memory. Then deliver Gold's framing line and hear whether it sounds honest or scripted.

Stage 8 — Pick an Option

Get a choice — not a purchase.

⚓ ANCHOR LINE:"Which option would you want to try and qualify for?"
🧠 WHY THIS MATTERSThis is the single most important idea in the script. You never ask anyone to buy. You ask which one they'd want to try to qualify for — which is a much smaller thing to say yes to, and it pays off the promise you made back in Stage 2. Understand this one mechanism and the whole script makes sense.

If they can't decide: ask permission to suggest, then recommend Silver or Bronze — we can always add coverage later but we can't decrease it. Then ask what's comfortable not just now but in 2, 5, 10 years.

The bridge: know immediately · no medical exam · green light or explore other options.

The objection that lives here: "I want to think about it"

🧠 WHY THIS MATTERSLearn the diagnosis before the responses. This lands right after quotes. In their head it's about price. What it usually means is value didn't get built well enough in Stage 4 or Stage 7. Agents who understand that start preventing it instead of just answering it.

Five responses, in escalating order — a ladder, not a menu. Work down only as far as you need. Most calls end at one or two.

1The company has to think about you too — let's see if you'd even be approved
2No commitment today, and you get 30 days to review the policy
3The shopping-around rebuild — I already did the underwriting across every A-rated carrier
4"What were you hoping to hear from another insurance company?" then silence
5These are just numbers on paper — let's make them real
HOW PEOPLE BLOW ITRung 4 is followed by silence, and nearly every new agent fills it. You ask the question and you stop talking, however long it takes. They will tell you what's actually wrong. Practice the pause on its own — time it if you have to, because it feels much longer than it is.

SELF-TEST

  • Run all five rungs from memory. Then have a partner give you "I want to think about it" and get out of it using only rungs 1 and 2.
PHASE 4CLOSE — Stages 9–10▼
lock it in
Job: complete the application and lock the sale in so it doesn't cancel.

Stage 9 — The Application

Confirm what you already wrote down, then get Social and banking.

🧠 WHY THIS MATTERSYou are confirming, not re-asking. DOB, height, weight — you already have these from Stage 4. Saying "you mentioned you were 5'10", just to confirm" sounds competent. Asking again sounds like you weren't listening, and it makes the call feel twice as long.

The two hard asks, and how they're handled:

Social
The moveThe simplified-issue paragraph does the work. No doctor, no blood, no cup — just an MIB prescription check. Delivered smoothly, the Social usually comes without a fight. Two backup versions if it doesn't.
Banking
The moveThey're verifying identity, not taking money. Say "no payments are due today" out loud — that's the actual fear underneath the objection.
HOW PEOPLE BLOW ITTreating the Social like a big deal. If you thank them twice or sound relieved, they start wondering if they should have said no. Take it, stay casual, keep moving.

Also captured here: beneficiary full name, relationship, and phone number · payment date · routing and account, read back to confirm · both authorization codes.

🧠 WHY THIS MATTERSThe two outcomes. Approved, or further underwriting. Learn both until either delivers without a hitch — the client reads any hesitation as bad news, so a pause before "further underwriting" makes a routine step sound like a rejection.

SELF-TEST

  • Deliver the simplified-issue paragraph in one smooth pass. Then deliver both outcomes back-to-back with no hesitation in either.

Stage 10 — Cement the Sale

Make sure the policy stays in force and the claim actually gets paid.

Four things happen here: the packet arrives in 7–10 days · your name goes on it in sharpie · the beneficiary gets told they're listed · they save your personal number.

🧠 WHY THIS MATTERSEverything in this stage exists to prevent two failures — a policy that cancels because nobody understood it, and a family that can't find the agent when they need to file. Neither costs you anything today, which is exactly why it's tempting to skip.
HOW PEOPLE BLOW ITThis is the most-skipped stage in the entire script, because by now you're excited the sale closed and it feels done. It isn't. A cancelled policy is a chargeback and a family with no coverage.

SELF-TEST

  • Name all four elements of Stage 10 from memory and explain what each one prevents.
DO THISThe call ends here. Referrals are optional and they come later — don't open that section until you're fluent through Stage 10.

the list

DRILLHow to Practice — In This Order▼
7 levels
1. Map
What you doRecite four phases, then ten stages, cold
Passed when…It's boring
2. Purpose
What you doSay what each stage is for in your own words
Passed when…You can explain any stage to someone else
3. Anchors
What you doDeliver the twelve key lines word-for-word
Passed when…No hesitation on any of them
4. Chunks
What you doRun one phase at a time, out loud, with a partner
Passed when…Each phase flows without the page
5. Full run
What you doStages 1–10 straight through, cooperative partner
Passed when…You get through without stopping
6. Hostile run
What you doPartner is vague, rambling, and objects at least twice
Passed when…You recover and still reach Stage 10
7. Certification
What you doFull run with a manager or agent lead, cold objection
Passed when…Cleared for live leads
WATCH OUTDon't skip levels. Most people want to jump to full run-throughs because it feels like real practice. But if you don't know the map, a full run just teaches you to read faster. Levels 1 and 2 take a couple of hours total and they make everything after them easier.
